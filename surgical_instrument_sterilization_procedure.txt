Sterile Processing of Surgical Instruments: Overview from OR to Sterility
Transport from the OR

Immediately after use, reusable surgical instruments are handled with universal precautions (gloves, eye protection, etc.) and prepared at point-of-use. Gross soil and fluids are wiped off or flushed out, sharps are removed, and instruments are kept moist (e.g. covered with a water-damp towel or sprayed with an enzymatic detergent) to prevent drying and corrosion

. Soiled instruments are placed in dedicated, closed transport containers or covered trolleys that are fully enclosed, leak- and puncture-resistant, and clearly marked with a biohazard symbol

. Containers must be decontaminated after each use, and sterile and soiled items are never mixed on the same cart

www3.paho.org
. Transport routes to the decontamination area are designated to avoid patient and supply areas, and carts are labeled as contaminated so staff are alerted to their contents
www3.paho.org
www3.paho.org
. Used sets are sorted and counted before leaving the OR so that all instruments can be tracked and verified against procedure logs (a key step for traceability and safety)

.

Point-of-use prep: Remove linens and disposable items; segregate sharps; wipe off gross debris; apply enzymatic spray or cover with moist towel (water only, no saline)
www3.paho.org
www3.paho.org
. Do not soak instruments in hypochlorite or disinfectants at the unit, as this can corrode metals and promote biofilm
www3.paho.org
.

Containment: Place instruments in dedicated transport trays or bins with lids or covers. These must be impervious and labeled (e.g. biohazard tag). Use tamper-proof locks or seals if needed for high-risk items
www3.paho.org
www3.paho.org
.

Routing: Send soiled loads promptly to the sterile processing department (SPD) by clean corridors, never through patient-care areas. Containers of contaminated instruments should not be pushed behind clean supply carts or mixed with sterile items

.

Decontamination (Initial Cleaning)

Upon arrival in the SPD dirty area, instruments undergo thorough decontamination to remove blood, tissue, and organic matter. This may be by manual cleaning, ultrasonic cleaners, and/or automated washer-disinfectors, depending on facility resources. All methods use mild alkaline detergents (pH ~8–10.8) specifically formulated for surgical instruments
www3.paho.org
. These detergents are used in warm water (per manufacturer’s directions) and must be mixed at the correct concentration
www3.paho.org
www3.paho.org
. Enzymatic cleaners (protease/lipase blends) are often used as a first step or soak to break down organic soils on instruments with heavy bioburden
www3.paho.org
. After this, mechanical action (brushing, flushing) physically dislodges remaining debris.

Manual cleaning: Immerse instruments in a basin of warm water with detergent; scrub all surfaces and internal lumens with soft nylon brushes and single-use cleaning cloths under water to minimize aerosols
www3.paho.org
www3.paho.org
. Use lumen brushes of the correct diameter and length to pass completely through channels
www3.paho.org
. Rinse and repeat until no visible soil remains. (Cleaning solution and rinse water are changed whenever they appear visibly dirty.)
www3.paho.org

Ultrasonic cleaners: These machines use high-frequency sound waves in a detergent bath to clean box locks, hinges, and lumens. Instruments must be pre-cleaned of gross debris first, then placed fully open and submerged. Water temperature is kept around 27–43 °C (never >60 °C) to avoid protein coagulation
www3.paho.org
. The tank is degassed (run empty) 5–10 minutes before use, then instruments are cleaned (typically ~5–15 min) and then thoroughly rinsed and dried
www3.paho.org
www3.paho.org
. The ultrasonic cleaner itself is drained and cleaned daily.

Automated washer-disinfectors: These are large machines with spray jets. A typical cycle includes a pre-rinse, an enzymatic wash, a detergent wash, and a final high-temperature rinse (often using demineralized water at ≥80 °C)
www3.paho.org
. These steps are precisely timed and heated to ensure reproducible cleaning. Loaded trays should not exceed machine capacity or obstruct spray arms. After the wash cycle, instruments are rinsed with clean water and dried inside the machine or manually.

Anti-biofilm protocols: Instruments must not sit in disinfectant (e.g. chlorinated solutions) during transport or cleaning, as organic debris will inactivate chemical agents and allow biofilms to form
www3.paho.org
. Instead, cleaning detergents (with or without enzymatic pre-soak) and mechanical action are relied on to remove all bioburden.

Cleaning and Rinsing Procedures

After decontamination, each instrument is inspected to ensure all debris has been removed. The cleaning process typically follows these steps:

Final brushing and flushing: With soap or detergent still present, re-brush all instruments (open scissors, flush cannulas with detergent solution, and clean any recessed areas) to dislodge residual soil
www3.paho.org
. Check lumened instruments for patency (no blockages) and leaks by flushing them with fluid or air.

Rinsing: Instruments are submerged in a clean sink or washer basin with fresh water and rinsed under flow or by immersion. Every surface and internal channel is thoroughly rinsed to remove detergent residues (which could interfere with sterilization)
www3.paho.org
. Ideally, potable water or deionized/sterile water is used (especially for sensitive lumens)
www3.paho.org
.

Drying: Complete drying is critical to prevent corrosion and microbial growth. Stainless steel instruments are immediately dried by wiping or blown-dry to avoid mineral spotting. Lumens and joints are dried by using medical-grade compressed air (filtered to HEPA standards) at a regulated pressure
www3.paho.org
. Air-drying or towel-drying with non-linting cloths is used for items that cannot tolerate pressurized air. All cloths are single-use and discarded.

After cleaning and rinsing, cleaned instruments are expected to be free of visible debris. Staff should perform a visual check (possibly with a magnifier) to verify cleanliness. All cleaning brushes and cloths are themselves disinfected and dried after each use, and replaced regularly to avoid recontamination
www3.paho.org
.

Inspection and Maintenance

Once cleaned, each instrument undergoes detailed inspection under bright light and often magnification (e.g. large lighted loupe)
aorn.org
. Key checks include:

Cleanliness verification: Inspect box locks, serrations, and lumens for any residual soil. Tools like borescopes may be used for internal channels in complex instruments to ensure no debris remains.

Functional test: Verify moving parts. Hinges and ratchets must open/close smoothly; scissors and forceps must align properly and lock securely. Telescopes, light cables, and other optical devices are checked for clarity and connectivity
www3.paho.org
. Cannulated devices are flushed again to confirm all channels are patent.

Damage assessment: Look for cracks, nicks, corrosion, pitting, or missing components. Any damaged, bent, incomplete or malfunctioning instrument is immediately taken out of service and reported for repair
www3.paho.org
. (Damaged surfaces can harbor bacteria and pose a safety risk.)

Lubrication: Hinged instruments (scissors, clamps, etc.) are lubricated with a water-soluble, biocompatible instrument lubricant (“instrument milk”)
www3.paho.org
. Only lubricants cleared for surgical instruments and compatible with the chosen sterilization method (especially steam) are used
www3.paho.org
. Oils or non-sterile greases are avoided because they can interfere with sterilization.

This assembly/inspection area may use a barrier (ISO Class 7 clean environment), and instruments are laid out on sterilization trays or assembly tables in single layers. Computerized tracking systems often record the inspection by lot number and technician. Many facilities perform spot tests (e.g., ATP assays) to verify that cleaned instruments are truly free of organic residue
aorn.org
.

Packaging for Sterilization

Once inspected and assembled, instruments are packaged to create a sterile barrier. Packaging choices include sterilization wraps (woven cotton or nonwoven polypropylene), disposable pouches (paper/plastic), tray liners, or rigid containers with filters
cdc.gov
www3.paho.org
. The packaging must allow sterilant penetration and maintain sterility until use. General guidelines are:

Instrument arrangement: Hinged instruments are left open; removable parts are detached unless manufacturer IFU say otherwise
cdc.gov
. Items with grooves or concave surfaces are placed to allow drainage. Heavy items are on the bottom of trays to prevent crushing delicate instruments above. Instruments are not stacked; trays are packed in a single layer to avoid airflow obstruction
cdc.gov
.

Packaging techniques: For wraps, the recommended double-wrapping or pouch folding methods (e.g. envelope or square fold) are used to create a sterile barrier
www3.paho.org
. Pouches with heat seals or peel-open packs are sealed securely (often with sterilization indicator tape)
www3.paho.org
. Rigid containers are lidded and have gasket seals and filter vents; loads in containers are wrapped or arranged per container IFU
www3.paho.org
.

Labeling: Each pack or container is labeled on the outside with the contents, sterilization date/lot, load number, and the initials of the packer/operator
www3.paho.org
. An external chemical integrator strip (or tape with chemical indicator) is applied to confirm that the required temperature/time exposure occurred
www3.paho.org
. Packages are kept closed and should remain unopened until used in the OR.

Packaging materials are stored in a clean area (18–22 °C, 35–70% RH) to avoid humidity damage
www3.paho.org
. All materials (wraps, filters, containers) must be validated for the sterilization process in use (steam, EtO, etc.)
www3.paho.org
. Once packed, trays are moved promptly to the sterilizer load area using dedicated carts (to avoid recontamination).

Sterilization Processes

Sterilization destroys all microbial life on instruments. The choice of sterilizer depends on instrument compatibility:

Steam sterilization (Autoclaving): The gold standard for heat-stable surgical instruments. Packaged loads are sterilized in saturated steam under pressure in autoclaves. Typical validated cycles are 121 °C for 30 minutes (gravity displacement load) or 132 °C for 4 minutes (pre-vacuum sterilisers)
cdc.gov
. The sterilizer type is chosen by the load: gravity cycles for non-porous loads, vacuum cycles for wrapped or heavy sets. Steam must contact all surfaces; air is removed via vacuum or gravity displacement prior to exposure
cdc.gov
. Each sterilizer cycle is monitored with physical (temperature/pressure/time), chemical (integrator strips or tapes), and biological indicators (spore tests). For example, a pre-vacuum cycle also requires a daily Bowie-Dick test to ensure air removal
cdc.gov
cdc.gov
. Successful sterilization is confirmed by negative microbial spore challenge (BI) and color-change CI in each load
www3.paho.org
cdc.gov
.

Low-temperature sterilization: For heat- or moisture-sensitive items (plastics, delicate optics), chemical methods are used. Common systems include:

Hydrogen peroxide gas plasma: A 50–60 L chamber is evacuated, vaporized hydrogen peroxide (e.g. 6 mg/L) diffuses for ~50 min, then an electrical field generates plasma, producing free radicals that inactivate microbes
cdc.gov
. Cycle conditions are mild (37–44 °C) and total cycle time is ~50–75 min (modern units down to ~28–38 min)
cdc.gov
cdc.gov
. Excess vapor is removed, and byproducts are just water and oxygen (no aeration needed)
cdc.gov
. Only dry loads can run (moisture aborts the cycle)
cdc.gov
. Biological indicators for this process are typically Bacillus atrophaeus spores
cdc.gov
.

Ethylene Oxide (EtO): EtO gas penetrates all materials and is used for bulk sterilization of mixed loads. A typical EtO cycle has five phases (humidify, gas injection, exposure, evacuation, multiple air washes) and runs 1–6 hours at ~37–63 °C, 40–80% humidity
cdc.gov
. After the sterilization phase, loads undergo extended aeration (often 8–12 hours at 50–60 °C) to desorb toxic EtO residue
cdc.gov
. Modern EtO chambers combine sterilization and aeration; unit-dose EtO cartridges are an alternative. EtO is monitored by indicators specific to EtO exposure, and any change in process (load content, material type) requires requalification.

Other methods: Peracetic acid immersion and vaporized hydrogen peroxide systems exist for endoscopes, but steam, EtO, and H₂O₂ plasma cover most applications. Each system has its own cycle parameters and must be used per manufacturer protocols. All sterilization cycles (steam or chemical) require strict validation: upon installation and after major maintenance, sterilizers are qualified with biological spore tests under empty-load conditions. Routine quality control mandates BI testing of every load containing implants and at least weekly testing for other loads, plus daily BI/CIs for general loads
www3.paho.org
. Chemical indicator readings are checked for every pack, and a log is kept of all sterility tests.

Storage and Distribution

Once sterilized and cooled, instrument trays are transferred to a sterile storage area. This area is a controlled clean zone with limited access; environmental parameters are maintained (typically 20–24 °C, ≤60–70% relative humidity)
cdc.gov
. Shelving is designed for single-layer trays or upright pouches to avoid crushing packages
www3.paho.org
. Sterile packs are managed by a first-in, first-out system to ensure oldest sterilizations are used first
www3.paho.org
. Each package bears a sterilization date and often a use-by date (or event-related notation) so that expired packs are identified and not used
www3.paho.org
www3.paho.org
.

Stored sets are periodically inspected for dust, moisture, or package damage. Any compromised package (tears, wetness, seal failure) is opened, and instruments are cleaned and reprocessed
jointcommission.org
. Staff use inventory or instrument-tracking systems (barcodes or RFID) to log each set and monitor sterilization status and upcoming surgical needs. Before a surgery, a sterile supply cart is assembled with the required sets and spares. After use, instrument sets are returned for reprocessing; many facilities record which case each set went to, ensuring traceability back to patient care
www3.paho.org
aorn.org
.

In summary, modern sterile processing is a multistep, tightly controlled workflow. Best practices globally emphasize early gross soil removal, meticulous cleaning (manual and automated), rigorous inspection, validated packaging and sterilization, and controlled storage. Throughout, documentation (traceability, load records, test logs) ensures every instrument is accounted for and safe for reuse
